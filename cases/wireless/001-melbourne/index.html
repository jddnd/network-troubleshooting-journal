Investigating Client Roaming and RF Overlap on Cisco Catalyst 9800

Notes from troubleshooting intermittent Wi-Fi interruptions during Teams calls on a small three-AP office deployment.

Context

I was troubleshooting reports of intermittent Wi-Fi interruption during Microsoft Teams calls in the Melbourne office. The visible symptom looked like a wireless drop, but that still left several possible fault domains: client radio/driver, roaming, RF coverage and overlap, AP/controller behavior, 802.1X/RADIUS, and EAP-TLS.

The purpose of these notes is to record the checks that were useful and the behavior I observed. The investigation is still being monitored after an RF change, so this is not presented as a final root-cause write-up.

What I wanted to understand

  • Were the call interruptions actually related to Wi-Fi roaming?
  • Were clients using 802.11r Fast Transition when they moved between APs?
  • Was ISE/RADIUS adding delay or failing during those transitions?
  • Was the Intel AX211 driver responsible for clients not selecting the FT AKM?
  • Were the three AP cells overlapping strongly enough to encourage unnecessary roaming?

What I checked

RADIUS / ISE health

The controller counters did not show a RADIUS transport problem:

CounterObserved
Authentication requests143,673
Timeouts0
Failovers0
Retransmissions0
802.1X transactions17,878 total / 17,876 success / 2 failure

This made ISE/RADIUS instability unlikely as the primary fault domain. One important lesson here: a large number of ISE authentication entries does not automatically mean a large number of hard disconnects. Roams and authentication-state transitions can create entries as well.

Whether FT actually works on the WLAN

One Melbourne client showed genuine fast roaming:

Roaming = True
11r = True
Fast roam = True
AKM = FT-DOT1X
Return to RUN: approximately 20–40 ms

That was enough to rule out a site-wide 802.11r configuration failure. The WLAN and AP infrastructure can perform FT.

A client that was not using FT

Another Melbourne client associated with the non-FT AKM and performed full 802.1X on each captured roam:

Roaming = True
11r = False
Fast roam = False
AKM = 802.1X-SHA256
Full DOT1X / EAP-TLS during roam

Two observed transitions were roughly 0.8–1.7 seconds. That is easily long enough to be noticeable in real-time voice or video.

Matched AX211 / driver test

I tested a same-generation Lenovo with Intel AX211 using the same Windows wireless driver version at a reference office. It still negotiated FT correctly.

Windows wireless driver: 24.20.2.1
Negotiated AKM: FT-802.1X

That ruled out the AX211 hardware family and that driver version as a sufficient explanation for the Melbourne client selecting the non-FT AKM.

Roaming frequency

The non-FT client was observed moving AP02 → AP01 → AP03 in roughly six seconds, crossing bands in the process. A separate FT-capable client was also roaming frequently. That shifted the question from only “how expensive is a roam?” to also “why are these clients roaming this often?”

RF observations

The 5 GHz radios were all running at 17 dBm on separate 20 MHz channels. 6 GHz was running at 10 dBm with 80 MHz channels.

5 GHz100 / 116 / 14017 dBm on all three APs
6 GHz5 / 37 / 8510 dBm on all three APs

The AP-to-AP 5 GHz neighbor measurements were the part that stood out:

Neighbor relationshipApprox. RSSI
AP03 ↔ AP02-41 to -42 dBm
AP03 ↔ AP01about -49 dBm
AP02 ↔ AP01about -51 dBm

Those are very strong neighbor levels for a three-AP office. Channel utilization was low, so the concern was not airtime congestion; it was cell overlap and the number of strong roaming candidates available to a client.

What I tried

I made one RF change only: reduce the maximum 5 GHz transmit power from 17 dBm to 14 dBm. I left 6 GHz, channels, WLAN security, FT and ISE unchanged so the result would be easier to interpret.

5 GHz max Tx power
17 dBm  →  14 dBm

6 GHz: unchanged
Channels: unchanged
802.11r: unchanged
ISE / RADIUS: unchanged

After the next TPC cycle, all three 5 GHz radios converged to 14 dBm:

AP01  *4/8 (14 dBm)
AP02  *4/8 (14 dBm)
AP03  *4/8 (14 dBm)

Result so far

At this point I am comfortable with the following conclusions:

  • RADIUS/ISE availability is not showing signs of being the primary problem.
  • 802.11r works on the Melbourne WLAN; it is not broken globally.
  • At least one client is choosing non-FT 802.1X-SHA256 and paying the full EAP-TLS cost during roaming.
  • The tested AX211 driver version is not sufficient by itself to explain that non-FT behavior.
  • Clients are roaming frequently enough that roaming behavior itself deserves attention.
  • The 5 GHz cells have very strong overlap, which makes RF cell sizing a reasonable variable to test.

The 17 → 14 dBm change is now active. The next useful evidence is whether real client roam frequency and user experience improve without creating weak edge coverage.

Notes / lessons

  • FT and roaming frequency are separate problems. 802.11r can make a roam cheap, but it does not explain why a client roams excessively.
  • ISE logs need context. Authentication-success events should not be counted as disconnects without correlating them to WLC client state.
  • Check the negotiated AKM, not only the WLAN configuration. FT can be enabled on the SSID while an individual client still selects the non-FT suite.
  • Matched controls are useful. Reproducing the same hardware/driver combination at another site quickly removed the driver version as the main theory.
  • AP-to-AP RSSI is useful for cell-sizing checks. Very strong neighbor levels can reveal overlap even when utilization, noise and client RSSI otherwise look healthy.
  • Change one variable at a time. Keeping 6 GHz, channels, FT and ISE unchanged makes the 5 GHz power test much more useful.

Next checks

  • Compare roam frequency after the 5 GHz power reduction.
  • Watch client RSSI/SNR at the weaker coverage edges.
  • Check whether the originally affected laptop negotiates FT or non-FT when it is next in the office.
  • For the non-FT client, force a clean disconnect/reconnect and verify whether it persistently chooses 802.1X-SHA256.
  • Compare the Windows WLAN profile and advanced adapter properties against a matched FT-capable control.
  • If another call interruption is reported, correlate the Windows timestamp directly with WLC client tracing.
  • Only after that, consider testing Melbourne-specific steering features individually if roam frequency remains excessive.
